From the jug to the tap

A researched plan to retire our 5-gallon water-delivery service and drink from our own tap — built for very hard Farmington Valley well water with a trace of uranium in a past test.

The short answer: retest the well first (our pipes were replaced since the last test, and CT DPH says that alone warrants a retest). Then install an NSF/ANSI 58 under-sink reverse-osmosis system with a remineralization stage at the kitchen sink, protected by a whole-house water softener. RO removes 90–99% of uranium and fixes both hardness and taste at the tap — and it pays for itself versus jug delivery in about a year.

Where we're starting

Why we're doing this

We stopped drinking our tap water years ago and switched to 5-gallon jug delivery. The water is very hard and doesn't taste good, and a private well test a few years back found a trace amount of uranium — below the allowed limit, but enough to make us cautious. Since then, the pipes serving the house have been replaced. The jugs work, but they're a recurring cost, a storage hassle, and — it turns out — no longer necessary.

The uranium is natural — and common

Uranium leaches from Connecticut's bedrock into well water. A USGS/CT DPH study found detectable uranium (≥1 µg/L) across roughly 45% of the state, with only ~4.7% of wells exceeding the 30 µg/L EPA limit. Below the limit, no treatment is required. The health concern at low levels is chemical kidney stress from long-term ingestion — not radiation. Levels fluctuate, so DPH advises retesting about every 5 years.

USGS study · CT DPH fact sheet

Our water really is "very hard"

USGS classifies anything over 180 mg/L (10.5 gpg) as very hard. Aquarion's public wells in Simsbury — drawing on the same aquifer local private wells tap — average 13 gpg (range 4–25). Even the utility softens two of its wells. Hardness is what causes the mineral taste, the scale, and — left untreated — clogged RO membranes.

USGS hardness scale · Aquarion Simsbury report

The taste is fixable

Calcium and magnesium above ~7 gpg give water a mineral edge; iron and manganese — common in wells — add metallic notes. RO strips all of it, but pure RO water tastes flat and is slightly acidic. The fix is a remineralization stage: a calcite cartridge after the membrane that adds back a small, pleasant amount of minerals and lifts pH to ~7.2–8.0.

The science

What actually removes uranium

Uranium travels in water as dissolved uranyl-carbonate complexes. Only a few technologies capture it — and two popular ones don't. CT DPH calls point-of-use reverse osmosis "the most common type of treatment used for uranium removal and very effective."

TechnologyRemoves uranium?Notes
Reverse osmosis (under-sink) ✓ Yes — 90–99% CT DPH's recommended approach for drinking water; membrane replaced every 2–3 years keeps performance up.
Anion exchange (whole-house) ✓ Yes — >98% Strong-base resin; the whole-house option if uranium ever approached or exceeded 30 µg/L. Overkill for trace levels.
Distillation ✓ Yes Effective but slow and energy-hungry; rarely the practical choice.
Carbon filters (pitchers, fridge, faucet) ✗ No Not designed for uranium — UConn's treatment matrix doesn't list uranium among what carbon removes.
Water softener (cation exchange) ✗ No 0–70% depending on pH, degrading as resin loads with calcium — WQA says it cannot be relied on for uranium. It has a different job (hardness).

Uranium enters the body only through ingestion — not through skin or breathing — so showering and bathing are safe, and treating just the drinking-and-cooking tap is the standard, health-department-endorsed approach. Sources: WQA uranium fact sheet, UConn Extension treatment guide, CT DPH.

The recommendation

The setup that fits our house

Every authority we checked — CT DPH, UConn Extension, the Water Quality Association — converges on the same architecture for hard well water with trace uranium.

1

Retest the well first

Our test is years old, and CT DPH explicitly recommends re-running the basic panel after any repair or replacement of water pipes — which we've done. Uranium levels also fluctuate over time. UConn's Institute of Water Resources runs a subsidized mail-in panel (bacteria, nitrate, metals, hardness, lead, arsenic, uranium) that would normally cost $350+. Knowing today's uranium, hardness, iron, and pH numbers is what lets us size the equipment correctly instead of guessing.

$200–235 · UConn CTIWR kit
2

Whole-house water softener (NSF/ANSI 44)

A salt-based ion-exchange softener fixes hardness everywhere — taste, scale, appliances — and it protects the RO membrane: on very hard feed water, softening upstream can stretch membrane life from ~18 months to ~4 years. Salt-free "TAC" conditioners are lower-maintenance but only condition scale; for genuinely very hard water, true softening is the safer call. (Don't worry about the added sodium — the RO removes it at the drinking tap.)

$1,200–3,800 installed + $60–300/yr salt
3

Under-sink reverse osmosis at the kitchen (NSF/ANSI 58)

This is the jug replacement: its own faucet at the sink covering all drinking and cooking water, with a $20 quarter-inch line teed to the fridge and ice maker. Look for NSF/ANSI 58 certification with a radium 226/228 reduction claim (the closest certified radionuclide claim — no consumer RO carries a certified uranium-specific claim, but CT DPH endorses RO for uranium regardless) and a remineralization stage for taste.

$200–1,000 + ~$100–170/yr filters
4

Licensed install, then verify

Connecticut requires a P1/P2 plumbing or J1/J2 water-conditioning license for installers (verify via DCP, 860-263-6000). After startup, lab-test the RO's product water to confirm uranium is non-detect and TDS is low — then test before-and-after the system periodically to prove it keeps working. That before/after test is also the moment we cancel the jug service with confidence.

$200–500 plumber (or DIY the RO)

Why the Home Master is our pick: it's designed for well water, wastes the least (a permeate pump brings it to ~1:1 where typical tank systems send 3 gallons to the drain per gallon made), its two-pass remineralization is the best-reviewed for taste, and annual filter cost is lowest. If end-to-end certification matters most, the iSpring RCC7AK at ~$200 or the Aquasana SmartFlow are the certified picks. Whichever we choose, the softener upstream (step 2) is what keeps the membrane alive on our water.

The money

Jugs are the expensive option

Our delivery service runs roughly $480–780 a year for a family's drinking water. Every filtration option beats it within a few years — the DIY route beats it within months.

Next steps

The action plan

  • Order the UConn CTIWR well test kit ($200–235, includes uranium and hardness) — or use a CT-certified lab like Phoenix Environmental in Manchester, (860) 645-1102. CT DPH Private Well Program can advise: (860) 509-7296.
  • Also test radon in air/water — radon comes from the same bedrock uranium chain, and CT DPH links the two; a uranium detection is a nudge to check radon too.
  • Get 2–3 softener quotes from licensed (P1/P2 or J1/J2) installers, sized to the tested hardness number — not to a salesperson's estimate.
  • Buy and install the under-sink RO (pick from the table above), with the fridge/ice-maker tee kit.
  • Lab-test the RO's output water — confirm uranium non-detect and low TDS.
  • Cancel the jug delivery service. 🎉
  • Recurring: RO filters every 6–12 months, membrane every 2–3 years, softener salt monthly, re-test uranium/arsenic/radon every ~5 years and the basic panel annually.
