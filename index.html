The short answer: get a proper baseline first — a certified mail-in lab panel (Tap Score Advanced City Water, $299) plus a PFAS kit, with a first-draw lead sample since our service pipes were recently replaced. Then install an under-sink reverse-osmosis system with a remineralization stage — the only under-sink category that fixes hardness taste, chlorine, and uranium at the tap. Where we're starting

What's actually in our water

We're on Aquarion's Simsbury system — public, regulated, and drawn from eight local groundwater wells. That's good news: the utility publishes a full water-quality report every year, and most of it is reassuring. But it also explains exactly why the tap tastes the way it does, and it leaves two questions only a test at our own tap can answer.

Mostly monitored, mostly fine

Aquarion's 2025 Simsbury report shows uranium at trace levels (max 2.3 ppb against a 30 ppb limit — consistent with our old private test), and disinfection byproducts far below limits. The chlorine residual (0.29–1.60 ppm) that keeps the system safe is also part of the taste we don't love.

Aquarion 2025 Simsbury report

The hardness is the local geology

Because the system pumps groundwater from the same aquifer as local wells, it averages 10 gpg of hardness (range 4–16) — hard to very hard on the USGS scale, and Aquarion softens two of its own wells to knock it down. Calcium and magnesium at these levels are the mineral edge in the taste, and the scale on our fixtures.

USGS hardness scale · 2025 report

What the utility report can't tell us

Two things are tap-specific. Lead and copper: our service pipes were recently replaced, and disturbed pipe scale can cause a temporary lead spike — in 2025 sampling, one Simsbury home exceeded the 15 ppb action level. PFAS: PFOA and PFOS were detected at up to 4 ppt — exactly at EPA's new limit. Both are questions for a test at our kitchen faucet.

Sampling tip that matters for us: collect the lead/copper sample as a first draw — first water out of the tap after 6+ hours of no use — because that's the worst case, and it's the number that reflects our recently replaced service lines. Hard-water fine print: at ~10–16 gpg, hardness will shorten an RO membrane's life (expect changes every 1–2 years instead of 2–5) — city water is gentler on membranes than raw well water, but it's worth adding a $15–40 polyphosphate scale-inhibitor prefilter as cheap insurance. A whole-house softener remains a worthwhile separate project for pipes, the water heater, and appliances — and it would extend the RO membrane's life too — but it isn't required to make the drinking-water plan work. The science

What actually removes uranium

At 2.3 ppb the system is far below the 30 ppb limit — but if the goal is "as close to zero as possible in our glasses," only some technologies get there. Uranium travels as dissolved uranyl-carbonate complexes, and CT DPH calls point-of-use reverse osmosis "the most common type of treatment used for uranium removal and very effective."

TechnologyRemoves uranium?Notes
Reverse osmosis (under-sink) ✓ Yes — 90–99% The health-department-endorsed approach for drinking water; membrane replaced on schedule keeps performance up.
Anion exchange (whole-house) ✓ Yes — >98% Strong-base resin; the whole-house option if uranium were ever near the limit. Overkill at trace levels.
Distillation ✓ Yes Effective but slow and energy-hungry; rarely the practical choice.
Carbon filters (pitchers, fridge, faucet) ✗ No Not designed for uranium — UConn's treatment matrix doesn't list uranium among what carbon removes.
Water softener (cation exchange) ✗ No 0–70% depending on pH, degrading as resin loads with calcium — WQA says it cannot be relied on for uranium.

Uranium enters the body only through ingestion — not through skin or breathing — so showering is safe and treating just the drinking-and-cooking tap is the standard approach. Sources: WQA uranium fact sheet, UConn Extension treatment guide, CT DPH.

The recommendation

The plan, in four steps

1

Baseline test at our own tap

Order the Tap Score Advanced City Water test and the Cyclopure PFAS kit. Take the lead/copper sample first-draw after an overnight of no water use. This tells us our tap's real lead, hardness, uranium, and PFAS numbers — the four things the utility's system-wide report can't — and Tap Score's report literally maps results to filter recommendations.

~$378 total
2

Under-sink RO with remineralization at the kitchen

This is the jug replacement: a dedicated faucet at the sink for all drinking and cooking water, plus a ~$20 quarter-inch line to the fridge and ice maker. Tankless if we value cabinet space and low waste; tank-style if we want the budget route. Either way: NSF/ANSI 58 certification, a remineralization stage so the water tastes like spring water rather than lab water, and a polyphosphate prefilter for membrane protection on our hard water. Specific models compared below.

$200–1,000 + ~$100–170/yr filters
3

Install it — DIY or a licensed local pro

All the consumer systems below ship as DIY kits (2–3 hours, basic tools). If we'd rather hire it out, Connecticut requires installers to hold a P1/P2 plumbing or J1/J2 water-conditioning license — verify anyone via CT DCP eLicense. Verified local options are in the installer table below; a Valley plumber installing a system we buy ourselves is typically the cheapest pro route.

$0 DIY · $200–500 plumber
4

Verify, then cancel the jugs

After startup, a cheap TDS meter confirms the membrane is working day-to-day (product water should read a small fraction of tap water), and a follow-up lab test on the RO's output closes the loop on lead, uranium, and PFAS. That's the day the delivery service gets cancelled.

~$15 TDS meter + optional retest
The softener question. A whole-house softener ($1,200–3,800 installed, plus $60–300/yr salt) is a comfort-and-appliances decision, not a drinking-water requirement: it would stop scale on fixtures and the water heater, and stretch the RO membrane's life — but the RO handles hardness in the drinking water by itself. Our take: do the RO now, revisit the softener if the baseline test comes back at the hard end of the 4–16 gpg range.

Next steps

The action plan

  • Download Aquarion's free 2025 Simsbury water quality report (aquarionwater.com) — the system-level baseline costs nothing.
  • Order the Tap Score Advanced City Water test ($299) + Cyclopure PFAS kit ($79). Collect the lead/copper sample first-draw after 6+ hours of no water use.
  • Read the results against this page: hardness number → softener decision; lead present → RO or NSF 53 stage confirmed; PFAS detected → prefer the PFAS-certified models (Aquasana SmartFlow, AO Smith).
  • Pick the RO system — tankless (Waterdrop G3P800) or tank (Home Master TMAFC-ERP), with remineralization and a polyphosphate prefilter — plus the fridge/ice-maker tee kit.
  • DIY the install, or get quotes from 2–3 installers above — verify licenses on CT DCP eLicense first.
  • Verify the output — TDS meter weekly at first, and an optional lab retest of the RO water for lead/uranium/PFAS.
  • Cancel the jug delivery service. 🎉
  • Recurring: RO filters every 6–12 months, membrane every 1–2 years on our hard water (longer if we ever add a softener), retest at the tap every few years.
